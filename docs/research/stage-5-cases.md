# Stage 5 — real-world rulers for "how long until it is forgotten / lost"

Research date: 2026-11-21. Candidate list for editing down to 6–8.

## Method and limits (read this first)

Every case below was researched by web search on the research date, not written from memory. **However, no source could be opened and read directly.** The research session's network rules blocked every page fetch attempted (archiv.ub.uni-heidelberg.de, pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov, gwern.net, rune.une.edu.au — both by the fetch tool and by direct download), so I had only what the search tool returned: result titles, URLs, and short extracts from the indexed pages. Consequently **zero cases are "fully verified by reading the source"**. The `quote` fields contain text exactly as the search tool returned it as an extract of the named page; these extracts are usually verbatim but I could not compare them with the publication, they are sometimes sentence fragments, and page numbers are given only where an extract or index entry showed them. Where no extract of the source itself was returned, `quote` says "none" rather than offering a paraphrase. Before any figure is printed or relied on, someone should open the cited source and check the quotation; the bibliographic details (author, title, year, journal, DOI) were all confirmed against at least one catalogue or publisher record in the search results. Ranges are deliberately wide and mostly reflect the choice of start and end point rather than measurement error. Wikipedia was used only to locate sources and is never the cited source.

Each block has the fields: `name`, `kind`, `from`, `to`, `low`, `high` (years), `source`, `quote`, `caveat`, `disputed`, `checked`.

## Rulers

### 1. One human generation

- `name`: One human generation (average age of parents at the birth of their children)
- `kind`: ruler
- `from`: birth of a parent
- `to`: birth of that parent's (average) child
- `low`: 25
- `high`: 32
- `source`: (a) Jack N. Fenner, "Cross-cultural estimation of the human generation interval for use in genetics-based population divergence studies", *American Journal of Physical Anthropology* 128(2), 2005, pp. 415–423, doi:10.1002/ajpa.20188. (b) Richard J. Wang, Samer I. Al-Saffar, Jeffrey Rogers, Matthew W. Hahn, "Human generation times across the past 250,000 years", *Science Advances* 9(1), 2023, doi:10.1126/sciadv.abm7047 (open access, PMC9821931).
- `quote`: (b), as returned by search extract of the paper: "26.9 ± 3.4 years (SE), with males at 30.7 ± 4.8 years and females at 23.2 ± 3.0 years". For (a) no extract of the paper's own numbers was returned; a table in a 2012 PNAS paper (PMC3465451, Table 1) attributes to Fenner: hunter-gatherers (157 societies) female 25.6, male 31.5, both sexes 28.6; nation states (360) female 27.3, male 30.8, both 29.1.
- `caveat`: The sex-averaged figure clusters at about 27–29 years; 25 and 32 are roughly the female-line and male-line ends. The popular "20 years per generation" and "25 years" are both too short by these studies; Wang et al. also report a dip to about 24.9 years roughly 6,400 years ago, so early-marrying societies can run shorter. Mother-only figures as low as 23 appear in Wang et al.
- `disputed`: false
- `checked`: Search extracts of the PMC full text of Wang et al. and of the PubMed abstract of Fenner; Fenner's actual numbers seen only as cited in the 2012 PNAS table. Neither paper opened. I had remembered "about 29 for Fenner", which matches. Wang DOI given from memory of the standard form — verify.

### 2. The limit of living ("communicative") memory — Assmann

- `name`: Communicative memory
- `kind`: ruler
- `from`: an event witnessed by living people
- `to`: the point at which no one remains who heard of it from a witness in ordinary conversation; after this it survives only if it has been given a fixed form (ritual, text, monument, formal tradition)
- `low`: 80
- `high`: 100
- `source`: (a) Jan Assmann, "Communicative and Cultural Memory", in Astrid Erll and Ansgar Nünning (eds.), *Cultural Memory Studies: An International and Interdisciplinary Handbook*, Berlin/New York: de Gruyter, 2008, pp. 109–118. (b) Jan Assmann, "Collective Memory and Cultural Identity", trans. John Czaplicka, *New German Critique* 65, 1995, pp. 125–133, doi:10.2307/488538.
- `quote`: (a), fragment as returned by search extract of the Heidelberg repository PDF: "has only a limited time depth which normally reaches no farther back than eighty years, the time span of three interacting generations". (b), fragment as returned by search extract of a scanned copy: "does not extend more than eighty to (at the very most) one hundred years into the past" — the same passage equates this with "three or four generations or the Latin saeculum" (second phrase seen only in the search tool's summary).
- `caveat`: This is a limit on informal, everyday memory, not on what a society can remember: Assmann's whole point is that "cultural memory" (formalised, specialist-carried) is not bound by it. The figure is a theoretical generalisation, not a measurement. Note that three generations of about 27–29 years is the same span.
- `disputed`: false (as a rule of thumb it is very widely used; it is not an empirical law)
- `checked`: Search extracts only; neither text opened. The 1995 wording was returned from an unofficial scanned copy (Scribd), not from JSTOR. Exact page within each article not confirmed (my recollection is p. 111 of the 2008 chapter and p. 127 of the 1995 article — unverified).

### 3. The "floating gap" — Vansina

- `name`: Floating gap
- `kind`: ruler
- `from`: the present of an oral society
- `to`: the point, moving forward with each generation, beyond which accounts thin out to almost nothing before the richly told "time of origins" begins
- `low`: 80
- `high`: 100
- `source`: Jan Vansina, *Oral Tradition as History*, Madison: University of Wisconsin Press, 1985, pp. 23–24 (index: "Floating gap, 23–24, 168–69, 226").
- `quote`: As returned by search extract of a full-text copy, located on p. 24: "Because the limit one reaches in time reckoning moves with the passage of generations, I have called the gap a floating gap." Also returned from the same passage: "at the junction of times of origin and the very sparse subsequent records, there usually is a chronological gap."
- `caveat`: **The 80–100 figure here is Assmann's, not Vansina's.** As far as the extracts show, Vansina gives no universal number: he says the depth varies with how a society's structure reckons time. Assmann identifies the near edge of the floating gap with his 80-year horizon. I recall Vansina giving a worked example (the Tio of Congo, limit c. 1800 as told c. 1880) but could not confirm it by search — do not use it unchecked. The useful lesson for fiction is the shape, not the number: plenty about the recent past, plenty about origins, almost nothing in between, and the origin story absorbs or replaces what falls into the gap.
- `disputed`: false (the concept); the numeric range is borrowed
- `checked`: Search extract of a third-party full-text copy (dokumen.pub) and its index entry. Book not opened. Recommend shipping this merged with case 2.

## Origin forgotten

### 4a. The Roma — from leaving India to presenting themselves as "Egyptians"

- `name`: Roma: Indian origin no longer told (first reading)
- `kind`: origin forgotten
- `from`: departure of the founding population from north/north-west India — about 1,500 years ago by genome-wide dating (c. 500 CE); some accounts put the departure later in the first millennium CE
- `to`: arrival of Romani groups in western Europe in 1417–1422, where they presented themselves as pilgrims from "Little Egypt" (hence "Gypsy")
- `low`: 400
- `high`: 900
- `source`: Isabel Mendizabal et al., "Reconstructing the Population History of European Romani from Genome-wide Data", *Current Biology* 22(24), 2012, pp. 2342–2349, doi:10.1016/j.cub.2012.10.039. For the 1417 arrival and the "Egypt" self-presentation: Council of Europe, *Factsheets on Romani History*, "Arrival in Europe" (rm.coe.int/arrival-in-europe-factsheets-on-romani-history/16808b1908); the standard book is Angus Fraser, *The Gypsies*, Oxford: Blackwell, 1992 (not seen).
- `quote`: Mendizabal et al., as returned by search extract of the abstract: "the Romani diaspora descends from a single initial founder population that originated in north/northwestern India 1.5 thousand years ago" and "the European spread of the Romani people was via the Balkans starting ~0.9 kya". No extract of Fraser was returned.
- `caveat`: It is not established that the Roma themselves had forgotten India by 1417: the "Egypt"/pilgrim story may have been a deliberate and useful presentation, and one scholarly article found by search notes that chroniclers in Bologna in 1422 called them "Indians" among other names. What is solid is that no Indian origin was on record among them or their neighbours in later centuries. The low end (400) rests on a later departure date that I saw only in secondary summaries. The genetic date has wide uncertainty and dates a founder event, not a memory.
- `disputed`: true
- `checked`: Search extracts of the Mendizabal abstract (repository and PubMed records) and of secondary pages on the 1417 arrival. No source opened. Yaron Matras, *Romani: A Linguistic Introduction* (Cambridge UP, 2002), which I expected to cite for a linguistic departure date, could not be reached; no figure is taken from it.

### 4b. The Roma — from leaving India to outsiders rediscovering the origin

- `name`: Roma: Indian origin unknown until recovered by linguists (second reading)
- `kind`: origin forgotten
- `from`: as 4a (c. 500 CE, possibly later in the first millennium)
- `to`: 1782–1783, when Johann Rüdiger (1782) and Heinrich Grellmann (1783) published the kinship of Romani with Indian languages
- `low`: 780
- `high`: 1,280
- `source`: As 4a for the start. For the end: Ian Hancock, "Mind the Doors! The Contribution of Linguistics", in Damian Le Bas and Thomas Acton (eds.), *All Change! Romani Studies through Romani Eyes*, Hatfield: University of Hertfordshire Press, 2010 (seminar paper dated 2009).
- `quote`: none from the source itself. The search tool's summary of secondary pages gave: "In 1782, Johann Christian Christoph Rüdiger published his research findings, in which he pointed out the relationship between the Romani language and Hindustani."
- `caveat`: This measures when European scholarship learned the origin, not when the community lost it; the language itself carried the evidence the whole time. That is the interesting point for a novelist: the story was gone but the proof was in everyday speech.
- `disputed`: false (the dates of Rüdiger and Grellmann); the start date carries 4a's uncertainty
- `checked`: Search summaries of secondary pages only. Hancock's chapter appeared in results but its text was not read; exact volume details should be checked.

### 5. Norse Greenland — a small, remote colony from founding to disappearance

- `name`: Norse Greenland, settlement to abandonment
- `kind`: memory kept (the colony kept its language, church and ties to Europe for its whole life; it is included as a ruler for how long a small remote colony lasted, not as a case of forgetting)
- `from`: Norse settlement, 985 CE
- `to`: end of the Eastern Settlement, mid-15th century; the last written record is the Hvalsey church wedding of 1408 (the Western Settlement ended earlier, about 1350, or by 1400 on other readings)
- `low`: 420
- `high`: 480
- `source`: Marisa Borreggine et al., "Sea-level rise in Southwest Greenland as a contributor to Viking abandonment", *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* 120(17), 2023, doi:10.1073/pnas.2209615120 (PMC10151458). Background: Andrew J. Dugmore et al., "Cultural adaptation, compounding vulnerabilities and conjunctures in Norse Greenland", *PNAS* 109(10), 2012, pp. 3658–3663 (PMC3309771).
- `quote`: none exact. The search tool reported, of the Borreggine abstract, that Vikings occupied Greenland "from 985 CE to the mid-15th century", and of the full text that the Eastern Settlement was "likely abandoned in the mid-15th century" with radiocarbon dates indicating about half a century of occupation after the 1408 wedding. These are the tool's renderings, not confirmed verbatim.
- `caveat`: About 15 to 17 generations, a few thousand people at most, never fully cut off: ships, a bishop and trade linked them to Norway and Iceland for most of the period. So it shows a colony dying without forgetting. It is the opposite of the "lost colony that forgot Earth" pattern. Low = 985 to 1408; high = 985 to about 1460.
- `disputed`: false for the span (causes of the end are heavily disputed)
- `checked`: Search extracts of the PMC records for both PNAS papers. Neither opened. Volume/issue of Borreggine given from memory of the standard citation — verify.

## Skill lost

### 6. Writing in Greece — Linear B to the alphabet

- `name`: Loss of writing in Greece after the Mycenaean palaces
- `kind`: skill lost
- `from`: destruction of the Mycenaean palaces and the last Linear B tablets, end of the 13th century BC (c. 1200 BC)
- `to`: earliest Greek alphabetic inscriptions, 8th century BC (none older than c. 750 BC on the usual view)
- `low`: 400
- `high`: 450
- `source`: Susan Sherratt, "Visible Writing: Questions of Script and Identity in Early Iron Age Greece and Cyprus", *Oxford Journal of Archaeology* 22(3), 2003, pp. 225–242 (she cites L. H. Jeffery, *The Local Scripts of Archaic Greece*, rev. ed., Oxford 1990, for the alphabetic dates).
- `quote`: As returned by search extract: "the parallel, but rather longer, gap in Greece between the Linear B script which dies with the palaces at the end of the thirteenth century, and the first alphabetic writing attested in the eighth century".
- `caveat`: Linear B was a narrow bookkeeping skill of palace scribes, probably a few dozen people per palace; when the palaces went, the reason for the skill went too. It was lost in at most a generation or two, not eroded over centuries, and the 400–450 years is the length of the gap, not the time taken to lose it. Greek oral poetry meanwhile carried a blurred memory of the palace age across the same gap. On Cyprus a related syllabic script did survive. Some scholars argue for an earlier adoption of the alphabet (9th century or before), which would shorten the gap.
- `disputed`: false (the gap); the exact date of the alphabet's adoption is debated
- `checked`: Search extract only. The extract was attributed by the search tool to Sherratt and came from a file named "visible_writing.pdf"; I matched it to the 2003 OJA article by title and author from memory. **This attribution needs checking** — a second search for the exact sentence did not find it again.

### 7. The potter's wheel in Britain after Rome

- `name`: Wheel-made pottery in post-Roman Britain
- `kind`: skill lost
- `from`: disappearance of wheel-thrown pottery in Britain, early 5th century (Roman rule ended c. 410)
- `to`: reintroduction of the wheel, around 700
- `low`: 250
- `high`: 300
- `source`: Bryan Ward-Perkins, *The Fall of Rome and the End of Civilization*, Oxford University Press, 2005, chapter on the disappearance of comfort (the surrounding discussion of Britain is cited by others to pp. 117–120); the sentence is reproduced in the publisher's excerpt, OUP blog, September–October 2005 (blog.oup.com/2005/10/a_return_to_pre/ and neighbouring posts).
- `quote`: As returned by search extract of the OUP excerpt: "All over Britain, the art of making pottery on a wheel disappeared in the early fifth century, and was not reintroduced for almost 300 years."
- `caveat`: This is the strongest single ruler for "a working industry gone within a lifetime": the loss itself took a few decades at most, in a population of perhaps a couple of million, with no catastrophe wiping out the people. But it is the collapse of a specialised, market-dependent industry, not of a household skill — hand-made pottery continued — and Ward-Perkins's bleak reading is contested by archaeologists who see more continuity (new 5th-century coin finds are cited against total collapse). Compare Robin Fleming, *The Material Fall of Roman Britain, 300–525 CE* (University of Pennsylvania Press, 2021), which supports the collapse view.
- `disputed`: true (degree and speed of the collapse; the absence of the wheel for roughly three centuries is widely accepted)
- `checked`: Search extract of the OUP blog excerpt. Book not opened; exact page of the sentence not confirmed.

### 8. Tasmania — tools dropped after isolation

- `name`: Tasmanian loss of bone tools and fishing
- `kind`: skill lost
- `from`: isolation of Tasmania from mainland Australia by rising seas, about 10,000 years ago (figures from 12,000 to 8,000 are in use)
- `to`: bone points vanish from the archaeological sequence about 3,500 years ago; fish drop out of the diet between about 3,800 and 3,500 years ago
- `low`: 4,500
- `high`: 8,500
- `source`: Joseph Henrich, "Demography and Cultural Evolution: How Adaptive Cultural Processes Can Produce Maladaptive Losses — The Tasmanian Case", *American Antiquity* 69(2), 2004, pp. 197–214. Against: Dwight Read, "Tasmanian Knowledge and Skill: Maladaptive Imitation or Adequate Technology?", *American Antiquity* 71(1), 2006, pp. 164–184; reply: Henrich, *American Antiquity* 71(4), 2006, pp. 771–782.
- `quote`: Henrich, as returned by search extract: "from the beginning of the Holocene until European explorers began arriving in the eighteenth century, the societies of Tasmania lost a series of valuable skills and technologies" (the tool reported this as following "over an 8,000-year period"). Read's abstract, as returned by search extract: "loss of skills more likely relates to change in the mode of resource procurement or change in technology rather than a shortage of skilled, target individuals due to decrease in population size."
- `caveat`: Heavily disputed on every level: whether the items were "lost" or sensibly given up, whether small population caused it, and whether the Tasmanian toolkit was in fact impoverished. The population was a few thousand, isolated for some 10,000 years, and the changes come thousands of years after isolation — so if anything it argues that an isolated small society keeps its kit for a very long time. The 3,500/3,800 dates come from Rhys Jones's Rocky Cape excavations and were seen only in secondary summaries.
- `disputed`: true
- `checked`: Search extracts of Henrich's text (author-hosted and archive copies) and of Read's abstract on the Cambridge site. Neither opened. The page range for Read (164–184) is from my memory and was not contradicted but not confirmed. Dates for bone points and fish are from secondary heritage summaries, not from Jones's own publications.

### 9. Polynesia — the end of long-distance voyaging

- `name`: Decline of open-sea voyaging in East Polynesia
- `kind`: skill lost (or abandoned)
- `from`: colonisation of the remote East Polynesian islands in one pulse, about AD 1190–1290 (Society Islands earlier, about AD 1025–1120)
- `to`: marked decline in inter-archipelago voyaging after about AD 1450
- `low`: 160
- `high`: 425
- `source`: Janet M. Wilmshurst, Terry L. Hunt, Carl P. Lipo, Atholl J. Anderson, "High-precision radiocarbon dating shows recent and rapid initial human colonization of East Polynesia", *PNAS* 108(5), 2011, pp. 1815–1820. Barry V. Rolett, "Voyaging and Interaction in Ancient East Polynesia", *Asian Perspectives* 41(2), 2002, pp. 182–194.
- `quote`: Wilmshurst et al., as returned by search extract of the abstract: "then after 70-265 y, dispersal continued in one major pulse to all remaining islands A.D. ∼1190-1290." Rolett, as returned by search extract of the abstract: "marked declines in voyaging and interaction after A.D. 1450".
- `caveat`: Voyaging contracted; navigation was not forgotten. Regional sailing continued, atoll societies such as the Tuamotus kept long-distance contact, and a later sourcing study (Weisler et al., *PNAS*, 2016) is reported to show inter-island transfers into the 1600s, which would push the high end past 500. The colonisation chronology itself is contested by those who favour earlier settlement. Outlying islands (Rapa Nui, the Chathams, New Zealand) did lose contact with the homeland and kept it only as a named ancestral place in tradition.
- `disputed`: true
- `checked`: Search extracts of both abstracts (PMC; University of Hawaiʻi ScholarSpace). Neither paper opened. Low = 1290 to 1450; high = 1025 to 1450.

### 10. Damascus (wootz) steel

- `name`: Patterned wootz "Damascus" blades
- `kind`: skill lost
- `from`: last blades with the highest-quality pattern, "probably around 1750"
- `to`: reproducible re-creation published in 1998
- `low`: 200
- `high`: 250
- `source`: J. D. Verhoeven, A. H. Pendray, W. E. Dauksch, "The Key Role of Impurities in Ancient Damascus Steel Blades", *JOM* 50(9), 1998, pp. 58–64 (tms.org/pubs/journals/jom/9809/verhoeven-9809.html).
- `quote`: As returned by search extracts of the article: "The date of the last blades produced with the highest-quality damascene patterns is uncertain, but is probably around 1750." and "Unfortunately, the technique of producing wootz Damascus steel blades is a lost art."
- `caveat`: This measures how long the skill stayed lost, not how long it took to lose, and the authors' own explanation is that the smiths did not know which ingredient mattered: the pattern depended on trace impurities in ore from particular Indian deposits, so the craft may have died when the supply changed, not when the smiths forgot anything. Other metallurgists dispute the mechanism. A good model for "they kept doing exactly what their masters did and it stopped working".
- `disputed`: true (the mechanism; the approximate date is the authors' own hedged estimate)
- `checked`: Search extracts of the TMS-hosted article text. Not opened.

## Memory or skill kept

### 11. Aboriginal Australian stories of the sea rising

- `name`: Aboriginal traditions of coastal drowning
- `kind`: memory kept
- `from`: the last time the described coastline could have been seen, by comparison with the post-glacial sea-level curve
- `to`: recording of the stories in the 19th and 20th centuries
- `low`: 7,250
- `high`: 13,070
- `source`: Patrick D. Nunn and Nicholas J. Reid, "Aboriginal Memories of Inundation of the Australian Coast Dating from More than 7000 Years Ago", *Australian Geographer* 47(1), 2016, pp. 11–47, doi:10.1080/00049182.2015.1077539.
- `quote`: As returned by search extract of the abstract: "They appear to have endured since 7250-13 070 cal years bp (5300-11 120 bc)."
- `caveat`: The range is the authors' own, across 21 story groups; it is a claim, not a consensus. The historian David Henige is reported (in press coverage) to object that it can be neither verified nor falsified and that very few oral traditions survive unchanged over even short observed spans; a figure of roughly 800 years is reported there as a conventional upper limit for intact oral tradition, which I could not trace to a scholarly source. What survives, even on the authors' reading, is a single striking fact ("the sea used to be out there, and it came in"), not a history.
- `disputed`: true
- `checked`: Search extracts of the abstract from two university repositories (UNE, UniSC). Paper not opened. Henige's objection seen only via news coverage (Science News, Scientific American), not in a publication of his.

### 12. The Klamath and the eruption of Mount Mazama

- `name`: Klamath tradition of the making of Crater Lake
- `kind`: memory kept
- `from`: climactic eruption of Mount Mazama, 7,682–7,584 calibrated years before present (i.e. before 1950)
- `to`: story recorded from a Klamath elder in 1865 (per secondary accounts; published much later)
- `low`: 7,500
- `high`: 7,700
- `source`: Date: Joanne Egan, Richard Staff, Jeff Blackford, "A high-precision age estimate of the Holocene Plinian eruption of Mount Mazama, Oregon, USA", *The Holocene* 25(7), 2015, pp. 1054–1067, doi:10.1177/0959683615576230. Tradition: Dorothy B. Vitaliano, "Geomythology: geological origins of myths and legends", in L. Piccardi and W. B. Masse (eds.), *Myth and Geology*, Geological Society of London Special Publication 273, 2007, pp. 1–7; the story itself is usually given from Ella E. Clark, *Indian Legends of the Pacific Northwest*, University of California Press, 1953.
- `quote`: Egan et al., as returned by search extract of the abstract: "7682–7584 cal. yr BP (95.4% probability range)". For the tradition, no extract of Vitaliano or Clark was returned; an Elsevier encyclopedia chapter ("Archaeology and Volcanism", *Encyclopedia of Volcanoes* companion site) was extracted as: "Oral accounts of the eruption must have been transmitted through approximately 250 generations."
- `caveat`: The eruption date is solid; the link is not. The story (a battle between the sky spirit Skell and the underworld spirit Llao that destroys a mountain) fits an eruption, but the best-known version was recorded by a settler and retold in polished form, later volcanic activity in the region could have fed it, and a US National Park Service study notes that another version contains virtually no hint of volcanic activity. People were certainly there: artefacts lie under the Mazama ash. "250 generations" assumes about 30 years each.
- `disputed`: true
- `checked`: Search extracts of the Egan abstract (Hull and British Library records) and of secondary pages on the tradition. No source opened; Vitaliano's page range is from memory and should be verified. Douglas Deur's 2002 article in *Oregon Historical Quarterly*, which I hoped to use, did not surface.

### 13. The Vedas — a long text carried by voice

- `name`: Oral transmission of the Rigveda
- `kind`: memory kept
- `from`: composition of the hymns, conventionally about 1500–1000 BCE
- `to`: oldest surviving Vedic manuscripts, about 1040 CE (Nepal); oral teaching continues to the present
- `low`: 2,000
- `high`: 2,500
- `source`: Michael Witzel, "The Development of the Vedic Canon and its Schools: The Social and Political Milieu", in M. Witzel (ed.), *Inside the Texts, Beyond the Texts: New Approaches to the Study of the Vedas*, Harvard Oriental Series, Opera Minora 2, Cambridge MA, 1997, pp. 257–345. For the date: Stephanie W. Jamison and Joel P. Brereton, *The Rigveda: The Earliest Religious Poetry of India*, Oxford University Press, 2014, Introduction, section "Date and cultural context of the R̥gveda".
- `quote`: Witzel, fragments as returned by search extracts: the texts "must be regarded as tape recordings" and were transmitted "usually without the change of a single word"; the earliest surviving manuscripts are placed in "Nepal (c. 1040 A.D.)". No extract of Jamison and Brereton's dating was returned (a secondary page quotes them as saying the date "has been and is likely to remain a matter of contention and reconsideration").
- `caveat`: This is the best-supported case of very long exact retention, and it shows the price: a hereditary class of specialists, years of full-time training per pupil, and elaborate cross-checking recitation patterns, all for a sacred text whose wording mattered more than its meaning (much of which became obscure). The composition date is inferred from language and content, not measured, and writing was in use in India for well over a thousand years before the oldest surviving manuscript, so "2,000+ years with no written support" overstates it; perhaps 1,000 years or more before any writing existed there at all. Counting to today gives about 3,000–3,500 years of continuous recitation.
- `disputed`: false for the fact of long, faithful oral transmission; true for the composition date (a minority argue for far earlier dates)
- `checked`: Search extracts of Witzel's 1997 essay (Heidelberg repository copy) only; fragments, not full sentences. The 1500–1000 BCE range was seen only in secondary summaries. Nothing opened.

### 14. Hebrew — a language kept without native speakers

- `name`: Hebrew between everyday speech and revival
- `kind`: skill kept
- `from`: end of Hebrew as an everyday spoken language, somewhere between about 200 and 400 CE
- `to`: revival as a spoken mother tongue, 1880s (Eliezer Ben-Yehuda's son, born 1882, is usually named as the first modern native speaker)
- `low`: 1,480
- `high`: 1,700
- `source`: Angel Sáenz-Badillos, *A History of the Hebrew Language*, trans. John Elwolde, Cambridge University Press, 1993, chapter 6 "Rabbinic Hebrew", pp. 161–201 (the decline of spoken Hebrew is cited by others to pp. 170–171).
- `quote`: none. No extract of the relevant passage was returned. A secondary page citing pp. 170–171 has him saying that less than a century after the publication of the Mishnah (c. 200 CE) Hebrew began to fall into disuse as a spoken language.
- `caveat`: When Hebrew stopped being spoken is disputed (dates from 135 CE to the 4th century are in circulation, and some specialists say the evidence cannot settle it). Hebrew never stopped being read, prayed, taught to boys and written in new works, by a dispersed but literate community with a strong religious motive — that is exactly why it survived, and why it is the only large-scale revival on record. A language kept only as liturgy, with no schools and no books, would be a different matter.
- `disputed`: true (the start date); the long survival is not in doubt
- `checked`: Search results only: the Cambridge Core listing confirmed the chapter and its page range; the claim attributed to pp. 170–171 was seen on a secondary page, not in the book. One search result gave the publisher as Columbia UP 1996; I believe CUP 1993 (paperback 1996) is correct — verify.

### 15. Ise — a building craft kept by rebuilding every twenty years

- `name`: Ise Shrine periodic rebuilding (shikinen sengū)
- `kind`: skill kept
- `from`: first recorded rebuilding of the Inner Shrine, 690 CE
- `to`: 62nd rebuilding completed, 2013 (63rd planned for 2033)
- `low`: 1,320
- `high`: 1,345
- `source`: *Encyclopaedia Britannica*, "Shikinen sengū" (britannica.com/topic/Shikinen-sengu); Kokugakuin University, *Encyclopedia of Shinto*, entry on the shikinen sengū (d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=9063).
- `quote`: As returned by search extracts — an English-language heritage description: "The Shikinen Sengū is normally held every twenty years and involves the ritual transfer of the sacred seat to a newly built sanctuary. The first recorded renewal took place at Naikū in 690."; Encyclopedia of Shinto: "the sengū ceremony was first performed for the Inner Shrine in 690 and for the Outer Shrine in 692."; and on the break: "the Shikinen Sengū was suspended for more than 120 years."
- `caveat`: Two rulers in one. The twenty-year interval is shorter than a generation, so every craftsman can take part once as a learner and once as a master — a deliberate design for keeping a skill. And the cycle broke for more than 120 years during the civil wars of the 15th–16th centuries and was then resumed: the skill outlasted a gap longer than living memory, in a literate society with records, surviving buildings of similar type and a continuing priesthood. How faithful the present form is to the 7th-century one is debated; I found no scholarly study of the transmission itself.
- `disputed`: false (dates); the claim of unchanged form is debatable
- `checked`: Search extracts of reference and heritage pages. These are reference works, not specialist scholarship, and which extract came from which page was not always clear. The exact years of the break (I recalled 1462–1585) were not confirmed. Nothing opened.

## Dropped

- **Roman concrete.** Good science on why it lasts (Jackson et al., *American Mineralogist* 102, 2017, pp. 1435–1450) but I found no solid source giving dates for when the knowledge was lost or regained; pozzolanic mortars were never wholly forgotten in Italy. A "lost for 1,300 years" figure would be invented.
- **Greek fire.** The usual dates (introduced c. 672, not recorded after 1204) appeared only in encyclopaedia summaries, which disagree with each other (668 vs 672). The scholarly treatment — John Haldon, "'Greek fire' revisited", in E. Jeffreys (ed.), *Byzantine Style, Religion and Civilization*, Cambridge UP, 2006 — could not be read. Also, "the recipe is unknown to us" is not the same as "they lost it".
- **Antikythera-class gearing.** Not researched for lack of time; from memory the gap to comparable geared devices is over a thousand years, but that is one object, and the claim depends on absence of evidence. Not sourced here, so not offered.
- **Latin into the Romance languages.** The end markers are well known (Council of Tours, 813, ordering sermons in the "rustic Roman tongue"; Oaths of Strasbourg, 842, earliest written Romance) but I saw them only in encyclopaedia pages, and there is no agreed start point: Roger Wright (*Late Latin and Early Romance in Spain and Carolingian France*, Liverpool: Francis Cairns, 1982) argues the Latin/Romance distinction was itself created by the reforms of about 800. Any span (from 350 to 850 years) would be mine, not a source's.
- **Sanskrit as a learned language.** Overlaps with the Vedas and Hebrew; not separately researched.
- **Budj Bim (Victoria, Australia) eruption tradition, about 37,000 years.** Erin Matchan et al., *Geology* 48, 2020 date the eruption to 36.9 ± 3.1 thousand years, and suggest Gunditjmara traditions may refer to it. The link is conjecture even by the authors' account; including it would put a 37,000-year figure on the list that almost no one would defend as memory.
- **Vansina's Tio example for the floating gap.** Remembered, not found; see case 3.
- **Stradivari varnish.** Excluded as requested.

## What the list as a whole suggests

Taken together, and with the warning that none of these sources was read in full, the cases point to a split and not to a single clock. Unrehearsed, everyday memory seems to run out at roughly three generations, about 80 to 100 years, and an origin can apparently go from known to legendary or replaced within a few centuries if nothing formal carries it (the Roma, on the weaker reading). A narrow skill that depends on an institution or a supply chain can vanish in a generation or two and stay gone for centuries (Linear B, the British potter's wheel, wootz steel), whereas broad subsistence skills seem far harder to lose, even over thousands of years of isolation (Tasmania, on most readings). Where a society builds a deliberate machine for remembering — a trained class, a ritual, a fixed text, a rebuilding cycle — retention of one or two thousand years is well attested (Vedas, Hebrew, Ise), and claims of seven thousand years and more exist but are disputed and concern a single bare fact, never a history. So the honest reading for an invented world is probably that the span matters less than whether anyone was given the job of remembering.
